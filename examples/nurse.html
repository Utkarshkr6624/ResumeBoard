Why this works

Eight decisions, each visible in the document on the left.

  1. Certifications sit above experience. A nurse recruiter screens for CCRN, ACLS, and expiry dates in the first ten seconds. Putting them at the bottom is how qualified people get filtered out by a keyword search.
  2. Every credential carries a date. An expired BLS is a hard stop. A date lets the reviewer confirm currency without a follow-up call.
  3. The promotion is shown inside one employer. Two roles under St. Aurelia tell the story of being promoted. Split across two companies, the same history would be invisible.
  4. Scope is stated in numbers a nurse manager recognises. Beds, ratios, preceptors a year, shifts covered. "Responsible for the care of patients" is not scope.
  5. Safety outcomes lead the list. A 38% fall in medication errors is the most persuasive line here, and it is the first one for a reason.
  6. The profile carries the current ratio and the current certifications. Those are the two facts a staffing office checks before anything else, and putting them at the top removes a phone call.
  7. Languages are graded, and the use is named. "Fluent, used for patient and family teaching" is a staffing advantage in its own right.
  8. There is not one personality adjective. No compassionate, no dedicated. Hospital hiring reads for scope and currency; warmth is assumed rather than claimed.

Adapting it

  1. Add your licence number and issuing state to the contact block if your region expects it. Recruiters otherwise ask on every single application.
  2. Use the photo slot only in markets that expect one. Many countries do, most US and UK applications do not, and sending one where it is unwanted reads as unfamiliarity.
  3. Keep the oldest role to one or two lines. Twelve years of nursing fits in half a page; the early jobs are there to show continuity, not to compete for attention.
