Why this works

Eight decisions, each visible in the document on the left.

  1. The summary states load, not stack. 40 requests a second, p99 from 1.4s to 180ms. A technical reader takes the number first; the language list is already further down the page.
  2. Numbers arrive in pairs. 1.4s → 180ms, fortnightly → daily, 12 pages → 3. A single figure with no baseline is a claim. A before and an after is evidence.
  3. Cost is treated as an engineering result. $420k a year is the clearest possible argument that right-sizing is real work, and it persuades far more people than "improved cloud efficiency".
  4. Work is described in things a non-specialist can picture. "A nightly batch that had taken four hours" lands with a hiring manager. "Refactored the ETL layer" does not.
  5. Each entry names what still exists. "Now run without me" and "the suite the team still runs" are evidence of quality. A deadline is not.
  6. Leadership is an output, not a title. "Two now lead their own teams" is checkable and modest. "Team leadership" is neither.
  7. On-call is on the page. 12 pages a week down to 3 is a fact about how the team works, and it is the line most senior resumes leave out.
  8. Skills are grouped by function, and the third group is unusual. Languages, systems, practice. Most resumes stop at the first two, which is exactly what makes a senior read like a senior.

Adapting it

  1. If your work has no number, name the shape: requests, users, rows, deploy frequency, pages a month, people affected. Scale is a metric.
  2. Lead with one stack. A page listing nine languages reads as a generalist, and the posting is looking for a specialist.
  3. Once you have three roles, consider cutting the oldest. Depth is what a staff-level conversation is actually testing.
