Why this works

Eight decisions, each visible in the document on the left.

  1. Education is the first section, not the third. A student resume is not a shortened professional one. The degree and the fact that you are enrolled are the strongest things on the page, and burying them under a part-time job reads as though you have nothing else.
  2. The graduation date is the most useful phrase you can add. "Expected May 2027" tells a recruiter whether they can hire you now, in June, or not at all. Without it, every application turns into a reply email asking.
  3. Ten hours a week is work. A lab assistantship, a tutoring shift, a barista job — all of it counts, and it sits under a section called "Relevant experience" rather than disappearing because it is not full-time. The label does the honest work: nothing here claims to be a software internship.
  4. The coffee shop is written for the job you want. "Took orders" is invisible. The same shift that trained four people and survived a 300-order afternoon is a line about training and throughput, which is the sentence a supervisor is actually scanning for.
  5. Two good projects beat six listed ones. Each project here names a constraint, not a feature list. Nobody remembers that a project used React; everybody remembers the four-year-old PDF nobody had opened.
  6. The small numbers are the honest ones. "About 15 hours a week", "roughly 300 orders", "three students". A student resume padded with only large numbers reads as marketing, and a reader who notices one inflated figure starts discounting the rest.
  7. The GPA stays because it is a good one. 3.72 goes on the page. A 2.6 does not, and no employer has ever asked a candidate to add theirs. Some graduate programmes do ask, and there it is not optional.
  8. The page is not padded to look full. This resume is one page and about two-thirds of it is text that carries information. Nothing says "hardworking team player", because a reader who reaches the bottom looking for it will conclude there is nothing underneath.

Adapting it

  1. Reorder before you rewrite. For a software internship, the research lab moves above the coffee shop and the tutoring entry can go entirely. For a non-technical internship or a campus job, tutoring stays and the projects section shrinks to one entry.
  2. Every project line should name the constraint rather than the feature. "Built a web app" is a feature list. "Wrote a parser for a four-year-old facilities PDF" is a problem someone solved, and that is what survives the interview.
  3. If your GPA is not one you want printed, delete it and lengthen the coursework line instead. The coursework is where your actual preparation shows, and it is the line a technical posting is scanning for.

For the general structure rules, see resume basics. For what a parser does with a page this shape, see how applicant tracking systems read a resume.

Questions this situation raises

  1. What if I have never had a job? A first resume built from education, coursework and projects is a normal document, not a weak one. Recruiters for internships and campus roles expect exactly that. Lead with the degree, the expected graduation date, and two or three projects described well, and leave out an objective that says motivated or hardworking, because it spends a line saying nothing.
  2. Should a student resume have a summary at the top? Only if it argues something. Two lines naming what you are studying, what you have actually built, and the kind of role you want is the useful version. A summary that restates your degree and calls yourself a motivated student is a paragraph of padding above the strongest section on the page.
  3. Does a student resume need a photo or an objective? No, in most markets. A photo adds nothing an employer can act on and slows some parsers down, and an objective restates the job posting back to the reader. The space those three take is better spent on coursework, projects, and what you did in a part-time job.
