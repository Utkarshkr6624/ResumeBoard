Why this works

Eight decisions, each visible in the document on the left.

  1. The summary does the translating, and it says so. Four sentences: scale, the domain, the specific problem the new field has, and why she wants it. A career change resume that skips this leaves the reader to do the arithmetic, and the reader will not.
  2. Every bullet borrows a word from the posting. Budget, forecast, actuals, rota, coverage, incident, audit, standard. Not hospitality words with a hat on — the nouns a nonprofit operations posting actually uses, attached to sixteen years of real evidence behind them.
  3. Nothing is renamed, and that is the point. The job title stays "Area Manager". Inventing a title you did not hold is the fastest way to lose an interview. The reframing happens in the bullets, where the reader is already looking for evidence.
  4. Weight follows recency, hard. One line for the 2009 role, three for 2012, five for the last eight years. A change of field makes this sharper than usual: what you did recently is the only part that demonstrates you are not starting from zero.
  5. Two pages is correct here. This is the one situation on the site where length is not a risk, because the history is real and the reader needs enough of it to judge the transfer. A career change resume cut to one page is cutting the evidence, not the padding.
  6. The skills block is written in the destination field's language. Four groups, four sentences, every phrase lifted from how the new sector describes its own work. A recruiter screens this block in seconds, and it is the one place where the translation is allowed to be blunt.
  7. Volunteering does the job a reference would do. Two years in the actual sector, on a rota, solving an actual waste problem. It is also the answer to the question the reader is actually asking: has she been near this work, or only thought about it?
  8. The one honest weakness is stated in the summary. "The constraint is funding rather than demand" is a sentence only a candidate without sector experience can write, because it is the actual difference. Naming it turns the objection into something she has clearly already considered.

Adapting it

  1. Write the destination field's own job description and count the nouns. Every one of them should appear somewhere in your resume. If you cannot place a required term anywhere, that is the real gap — and it is a thing to fix before the interview, not on the page.
  2. Do not start a resume with "I am making a transition from hospitality". It is a disclaimer, it puts your old field in your own first sentence, and it invites the reader to score you against a different ladder. Start with the capability and mention the field once, later.
  3. Cut the last role on the page, not the first. The oldest entry is the one whose detail adds nothing; the recent ones are where the transfer is visible. Stopping at about eight years back keeps the evidence and cuts the years you are least proud of.

Two pages is the only place on this site where the one-page rule does not apply, and the student example is the opposite problem: thin history, one page.

Questions this situation raises

  1. How much of my old career should I keep? All of the recent part, and progressively less of the rest. Two pages is acceptable here precisely because there is real history. Keep about a third of the page on the work from more than eight years ago, and rewrite its bullets in the language of the field you are moving into rather than the one you are leaving.
  2. Do I need to explain a gap on the resume? No. A gap that sits between two roles explains itself, and a paragraph on the page defending it invites more questions than it settles. If the gap contains something real, fill it with what you did in it: study, caring for a family member, a freelance or volunteer commitment. If it contains nothing, leave the space empty.
  3. Should I go back to school to change fields? Only when the new field genuinely gates on a credential, which is true for nursing, law, teaching and some engineering paths and false for most operations, analysis, product, and communications roles. A short certificate or a professional qualification is usually enough signal; a two-year degree is usually a way of avoiding the harder part, which is proving you can do the work.
