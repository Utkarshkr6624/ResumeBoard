Why this works

Eight decisions, each visible in the document on the left.

  1. The summary admits a deletion. "$86k removed, pipeline up 19%." Almost every marketing resume is a list of things done. The line that says you cut something is the one people remember.
  2. Every campaign is cost plus result. No impressions, no reach, no engagement. Those are inputs, and no hiring manager can tell good work from expensive work using inputs alone.
  3. The budget is stated as a budget. "$1.4M, forecast within 2% for three years" is a claim about competence. "Managed a large budget" is a claim about nothing.
  4. Retention outranks traffic. 54% to 71% at 90 days is a business outcome, which is why it sits above a growth figure from the same year.
  5. The early role stays small. One coordinator entry, two bullets, an honest title. A legible career shape persuades more than a tidied-up one, and the jump is the story.
  6. The award comes last. Recognition belongs after the evidence. One award in a nine-year career reads as credible in a way three would not.
  7. Skills are grouped by purpose. Planning, channels, tools. A candidate for a marketing manager role scans for channels first, so channels go in the middle rather than the bottom.
  8. Nothing is written about passion. Nine lines of numbers, and not one of them about loving the work. A hiring manager is being asked to fund a job, not to admire a hobby.

Adapting it

  1. Use the metrics your own reporting already gives you. If you have none, use spend, volume, or a deadline you hit on time.
  2. Name the segment. "B2B software, mid-market" is a filter a recruiter can apply in one second. "Technology" is not a filter.
  3. Cut the tools group if it runs past four items. Long software lists date fast and are rarely what a manager hires on.
