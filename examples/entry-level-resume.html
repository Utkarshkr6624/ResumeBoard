Why this works

Eight decisions, each visible in the document on the left.

  1. The summary is two lines, not a paragraph. It names a field (consumer debt), a method (reconciling, chasing a discrepancy) and a seniority level. At this stage the reader has no other evidence, so the summary has to carry a claim — but a claim, not a biography.
  2. One page, and the page ends early. There is no career history yet that would justify a second sheet. A two-page entry-level resume is almost always a longer description of a café job, and length is not a score.
  3. Each bullet ends in something checkable. "220 files, 31 flagged", "60,000 rows", "five new baristas". A first job rarely produces percentage changes, and the fix is scale and count, not a fabricated lift.
  4. The internship leads even though it is over. Most recent first is the rule, and an eight-week internship at a bank is more relevant to an analyst posting than two years of café work. Same rule, higher stakes at this level.
  5. One bullet per job is about something you left behind. The onboarding guide, the training checklist, the tagging change. Small, permanent improvements read as competence in a way that participation never does.
  6. Skills are grouped by the task, not the tool. "Reconciliation, exception review" tells a hiring manager what you would do on day one. A list of software names tells them what you have clicked.
  7. The café job is reframed around what a supervisor cares about. Closing the shop, training an intake, fixing a specific recurring error. None of that says barista, and all of it says the job was done properly.
  8. The gap is not explained, because there isn't one. The dates run continuously. Inventing a cover story for a period a reader can already see is empty, and it is the kind of thing that gets checked.

Adapting it

  1. Write one sentence at the top that repeats the posting's own nouns, then leave it. Junior analyst, junior data analyst, financial analyst — the entry-level market is thin enough that matching the title is a real advantage, and it costs one line.
  2. Cut the weakest role before you cut words from the others. Two strong entries and a one-line third beat three entries where the third is padding. If a job cannot produce a specific line, it may not belong on the page.
  3. For every job you keep, ask what changed while you were there and write that. If the honest answer is "nothing", say what you handled instead of what you improved.

Questions this situation raises

  1. How long should an entry-level resume be? One page, without exception at this level. There is no career history yet that would justify a second, and a second page mostly contains a longer description of a barista job. If you run out of material, that is a signal to tighten the wording, not to pad the page.
  2. I have no metrics from my jobs yet. What do I write? Write the scale instead: how many people, how many items, how often, how long. Trained four new staff members, handled three hundred orders in an afternoon, ran a survey of sixty responses. An honest scale is usable and verifiable in an interview, and inventing a percentage is not.
  3. Should an entry-level resume apply to roles one level above the posting? Yes, selectively, and only where you meet most of the listed requirements. The entry-level market is thin, so the first role above the title you want is often the realistic entry point. Apply to both, but reorder the summary and the skills block for the higher posting rather than sending the identical document twice.
