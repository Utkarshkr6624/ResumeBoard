Why this works

Eight decisions, each visible in the document on the left.

  1. The close is the headline. Twenty closes, every one before the fifth working day. That is the first number an accounting manager asks about, and it is the first bullet.
  2. Audits are stated with outcomes. Two audits, no material findings, no control deficiencies, and ownership of the preparation. "Assisted with audit" is the phrase people use when they were in the room.
  3. Time and money are both quantified. Nine days to four is a process metric; eleven revolver draws to three is a cash metric. Together they are the entire controller job in two lines.
  4. A past error is named honestly. The commission accrual that had been wrong in two of three years is a risk found and fixed. Every controller reading this has found the same spreadsheet, and admitting it reads as competence rather than as a gap.
  5. Standards are named where they matter. US GAAP, ASC 606, IFRS consolidation. "Financial reporting" is a category; those three are a filter.
  6. The credential carries a status. "Active since 2018" is what a hiring manager needs. A bare CPA leaves them wondering whether the licence lapsed.
  7. Systems are listed by depth. "NetSuite, five years in production" tells a controller more than a column of twenty software names.
  8. Client work is kept, even the unglamorous parts. A physical inventory that recovered $180k is real accounting work, and it is the kind of line that gets read twice.

Adapting it

  1. Name the standards you actually work under. "US GAAP" is a filter; "GAAP" is a guess.
  2. Convert process numbers into money wherever you can. Days saved is second-best. Dollars and interest saved is the version that reaches the CFO.
  3. State the issuing state and year of any licence you hold. Verification takes them one click, and its absence costs you the whole application.
