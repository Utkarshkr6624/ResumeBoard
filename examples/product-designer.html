Why this works

Eight decisions, each visible in the document on the left.

  1. The summary carries scope, not adjectives. Eight years, 2M+ customers, 18%. A recruiter decides in six seconds whether to keep reading, and these are the three facts that decide it. "Passionate and detail-oriented" is a waste of the line.
  2. Every bullet is a claim, not a duty. "Led", "built", "ran", "mentored" — then a number. If a bullet could be pasted onto someone else's resume unchanged, it is not doing its job.
  3. One number per bullet. Three metrics in a line means the reader cannot tell which one mattered. Pick the one that changed the business.
  4. Dates are consistent and reversible. "Mar 2021 — Present" everywhere, never "03/2021" in one place and "March 2021" in another. Inconsistent dates read as a proofreading failure.
  5. The summary names a specialty. Checkout, design systems, payments. Those are the words a hiring manager can match against a job description, and matchers are literal.
  6. Weight follows recency. Studio Mesa, eight years ago, gets two lines. A resume that treats 2016 and 2021 equally misleads about where your strength actually is.
  7. Skills are grouped by what they enable. Design, Research, Tools — capability first, software second. A recruiter looking for prototyping finds it without reading a list of applications.
  8. Education is one line, below experience. At eight years in, the degree is a formality. It is not the reason anyone called.

Adapting it

  1. Swap the two or three bullets that match the posting to the top of the first role. Order inside a role is the cheapest targeting there is.
  2. If you have no number for something, describe the scale instead: team size, users, budget, deadline. An honest scale beats an invented percentage.
  3. Cut the project and the certification entirely if they are not current. Both are optional, and a stale extra section costs you credibility.
