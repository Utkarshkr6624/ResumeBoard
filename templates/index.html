Template gallery

59 layouts, drawn rather than photographed

Every sheet below is live HTML and CSS at real page proportions, built from the same template registry the builder applies to your document. There is no screenshot anywhere on this site, because a screenshot of a resume template is a picture of a promise and the proportions are the whole point. Each one is filled with a fictional resume so you can see how it behaves full rather than empty.

The people are invented and the jobs with them. Every measurement on these pages is a value in the builder’s own registry or a size derived from it by the document stylesheet’s type scale — margins in inches, body text in points, section gaps in points. Nothing here is a pass rate, a ranking or a hiring outcome, because no applicant tracking system publishes one and neither do we.

The set

Read the line under each card as the summary and the card as the detail. 54 of these 59 are single-column layouts, and 5 are two-column pages that are worth looking at and are not worth sending to a parser. If your employer runs an applicant tracking system you will find part of that decision pre-made: the ATS guide explains why, and print-safe is built to survive one.

What a specimen is, and what it is not

These pages are static, so each sheet is drawn with the same values the builder writes onto the document — the same margins, body size, leading, gaps, rules, bullets, arrangement and page size, from the same registry file, through the same function. It is exact about geometry and type, and it is not a screen capture: the live document also has editing handles, selection outlines and page-break logic that are deliberately absent here. What the builder gives you that these cannot is a page that fills with your own text.

How to choose in about a minute

Four questions decide it, and only the first one is really about looks.

  1. Will an applicant tracking system read this file? If you do not know, assume yes. That rules out the two-column pages and narrows the field to the 54 one-column layouts.
  2. Is this a CV or a resume? Publications, grants and appointments mean you want academic or folio. If the word resume appears in the posting, they are the wrong page.
  3. What sheet do they print on? Three of these ship as A4 and the rest as Letter. Both are one click to change in the builder, but the default is what most people end up sending.
  4. How much do you have to fit? A long career, or a second page you would rather not have, is what compact or maximalist is for. Otherwise give yourself the room.

If you still cannot decide, classic is the layout most readers have seen most often, which is a low-risk reason to pick something. The registry calls it the safe default, and it is the one most people end up on without choosing.

What every template in the set shares

Each of these 59 is a set of design tokens plus, at most, a named arrangement. There is one document stylesheet on this site and changing a template swaps an attribute and repaints a handful of custom properties; the document is never rebuilt. That is why a preset is a dozen numbers and a word or two of shape rather than a page of markup, and it is why this index is generated from the registry: a template that does not exist cannot appear here, and one that does cannot be missing.

The consequences are worth stating once, because they are the rules the whole set is built against. One column unless a template says otherwise on its own page. Web-safe faces only, because nothing is ever downloaded. No tables, because a table is a layout a parser has to guess at. No text boxes, for the same reason. No icon glyphs standing in for bullets, because a pictographic marker is not text. Section headings that stand alone on their own line rather than sharing a row with the first entry. And an inter-entry gap that is visibly larger than the inter-bullet gap, so a reader can tell where one job ends.
