Guide 03

Action verbs: a scannable list for resume bullets

A resume bullet is a sentence, and like any sentence it needs a subject. The verb is the subject, it is the first thing a reader sees, and it is the fastest available signal of what kind of work you have done. This guide has the grammar, the weak verbs worth deleting, a categorised list of strong ones, and the tense rules that hold up.

Two sentences describing the same two years of work can look identical and rank completely differently, and the difference is usually one word. A resume is skimmed, not read, so the first word on the line is the one doing the work. Spend your effort there before you spend it on the rest of the sentence.

The grammar of a bullet

A resume bullet has three slots, and they are not optional.

The three parts of a resume bullet
SlotWhat it doesExample
VerbNames the action and, by implication, the level. This is the only slot a skimmed reader definitely readsRebuilt, cut, grew, diagnosed
ObjectNames what the action was applied to. This is where the specific noun lives, and specific nouns are what a reader is scanning forthe supplier onboarding process, a nine-tab spreadsheet, a queue of 400 open tickets
ResultWhat changed. Optional in about half of good bullets and present in the half that matter mostfrom nine days to two, saving four days a quarter, removing a recurring source of errors

The subject is implied: you. That is why the bullet starts with a verb rather than I, and it is also why responsible for is such a common opening. Responsible is a verb describing a relationship to work rather than an action performed on it, and it is the grammatical equivalent of a bullet that says nothing in the position the reader is looking at.

The same year, described as duty and as effect
Duty
  • Responsible for the reporting pack for the warehouse and the finance team.
  • Helped to improve processes around picking and packing.
  • Worked alongside the ops manager on stock counts.
Effect
  • Rebuilt the weekly reporting pack, replacing a nine-tab spreadsheet with a single sheet that finance ran themselves.
  • Cut picking errors by a third by moving stock checks from nightly to every second slot.
  • Counted stock for the two annual audits, both of which closed without a write-off.

Nothing was invented. The person had the same job in both versions. The second version is simply the first version after somebody asked three questions: what did you make, what did you stop, and how do you know. Those questions are worth more than any vocabulary list, and the list below is only useful for answering the first one.

Three tiers of verb strength

Verbs carry an implied level, and a reader uses that level to place you before reading the rest of the sentence. The tiers are real, and putting the wrong one at the front of a bullet misrepresents you more effectively than any adjective could.

Tiers of action verb strength and what each signals
TierWhat it signalsVerbs
Owning You had the outcome on your name, not just the tasks Owned · Led · Directed · Established · Introduced · Launched · Built
Moving You changed something and the change is the point Rebuilt · Grew · Cut · Migrated · Streamlined · Consolidated · Shifted · Recovered
Doing You carried out work competently, which is a perfectly good thing to say Coordinated · Delivered · Prepared · Maintained · Administered · Ran · Supported

The mistake is upgrading. If you coordinated a project that somebody else owned, led is a claim you will be asked about in the interview, and the answer will be that you were the only person in the room from a different department. Coordinated is accurate, it is specific, and it survives the follow-up question. A reader who sees led and hears well, it was a two-person thing discounts the rest of the page slightly. A reader who sees coordinated and hears the same thing thinks you described your job accurately, which is the best possible outcome.

The reverse mistake is understating. Plenty of people write assisted with or helped to about work they were solely responsible for, usually because they were junior when they did it. The fix is not to inflate; it is to describe the work and let the reader draw the level conclusion themselves. Set up the returns process for the online store is doing a job at a reader can interpret correctly.

The verbs worth deleting

These are the openings that appear most often on real resumes and tell a reader the least. The right-hand column is not always a single replacement, because sometimes the correct move is to delete the verb and start the sentence with the object.

Weak opening phrases and what to write instead
Instead ofWriteWhy
Responsible forManaged, ran, ownedDescribes an allocation of work rather than an action
Worked onBuilt, rewrote, tested, migratedConceals whether you did the work or attended it
Helped to / helped withRebuilt, drafted, ran, or name your partUsually means you owned it, or that nobody can tell
Involved inDelivered, audited, organised, presentedEverybody is involved in something. Almost nobody is
Utilised / leveragedUsed, with the thing namedCorporate gloss on a plain word, and it hides the object
SpearheadedLed, started, set upSays nothing except that the writer enjoys Latinate words
Synergised withWorked with, joined, partneredNonsense as a verb in this context, and it obscures the actual work
Tasked withDelivered, ran, wrote, fixedPassive framing for something you clearly did
Fulfilled dutiesDelete the phrase and list what you didDuty is what the job description already said
Acted asWas, or name the role: ran the Tuesday clinicMakes a permanent job sound like a favour
Duties includedDelete it. Start the bulletsAnnounces that what follows is a list of tasks, which is the least interesting possible framing
Took part inAttended, contributed to, presented atWeak, and slightly suggests a room you were not contributing in

There is one genuine exception. Supported, assisted and contributed to are correct when the work really was shared, and on a first job with a small team they are perfectly respectable. The problem is not accuracy. It is that these words are also the standard hiding place for work you are not sure how to describe, and they are worth using only after you have checked you cannot do better.

The verb list, by category

Find the row that describes the work, then pick the verb inside it that is the most accurate, not the most impressive. If two words fit, the plainer one is usually the better one, because the object after it is doing the real work anyway.

Action verbs grouped by the kind of work they describe
Kind of workVerbs
Making something exist Built · Designed · Developed · Engineered · Implemented · Prototyped · Assembled · Deployed · Shipped · Launched · Set up · Created
Changing something Rebuilt · Rewrote · Replaced · Migrated · Refactored · Overhauled · Redesigned · Restructured · Consolidated · Simplified · Modernised
Increasing a number Grew · Increased · Expanded · Scaled · Drove · Generated · Acquired · Converted · Attracted · Lifted · Won · Doubled
Decreasing a number Cut · Reduced · Eliminated · Halved · Removed · Shortened · Cleared · Brought down · Lowered · Dealt with
Running a process Managed · Ran · Operated · Owned · Administered · Delivered · Oversaw · Maintained · Coordinated · Scheduled · Dispatched · Served
Getting a result out of people Led · Directed · Chaired · Mentored · Coached · Trained · Onboarded · Introduced · Supervised · Delegated · Hired · Retained
Working out what was wrong Diagnosed · Investigated · Identified · Traced · Analysed · Forecast · Modelled · Benchmarked · Segmented · Evaluated · Assessed · Mapped
Fixing and keeping it fixed Resolved · Recovered · Stabilised · Repaired · Upgraded · Patched · Tuned · Standardised · Documented · Audited · Reconciled · Validated
Talking to people Advised · Briefed · Negotiated · Presented · Wrote · Published · Taught · Explained · Answered · Handled · Championed · Represented
Making something happen Coordinated · Facilitated · Arranged · Negotiated · Mobilised · Launched · Concluded · Submitted · Sourced · Procured · Budgeted · Forecast

Two notes on the list. First, spelling: this page uses British forms, and an American resume will use analyzed, standardized, mobilized. Pick one and stay with it, because a document that mixes organised with organized reads as though two people wrote it. The one exception worth making is proper nouns, which are always spelled the way the company or product spells itself.

Second, some of these verbs overlap on purpose. Forecast appears twice because it fits both an analytical role and an operations one. Negotiated appears twice because it is genuinely a skill, not a category. Overlap is a feature of a list you are meant to scan, not a defect in it.

Tense, and the one rule

There is exactly one tense rule on a resume, and it is not optional.

The tense rule
  • Your current role is in the present tense.
  • Every role before it is in the past tense.
  • Do not mix them inside a single entry, and do not start a current job in the past.
Which tense to use in each part of a resume
WhereTenseLooks like
Current role, ongoing workPresentManage the supplier onboarding process for three sites
Current role, completed projectsPresent for the bullet, past for the finished thingRebuilt the reporting pack in 2024 and now maintain it
Every previous rolePastManaged the returns desk for the online store
Education, dates, headingsNo tenseUniversity of Leeds, BA Economics, 2014–2017
Skills linesBare infinitive or presentProficient in · Experienced with · Fluent in

The rule is small but it does real work, because tense is a free signal of where your attention is. A current job described in the past tense reads as though you have already left, and a fifteen-year-old job described in the present tense reads as an error. Recruiters notice both without being able to say why.

One awkward case: a job you are leaving in a month. Still write it in the present tense and use an explicit end date. Present means the work is current as of the date on the document, not that you intend to stay forever.

Matching the posting

Here is a method that takes ten minutes and is more reliable than any list of "resume keywords."

  1. Print or copy the job description. Not the title. The body, in full.
  2. Highlight every verb in it. Words like manage, build, analyse, negotiate, deliver. A posting written by someone who has done the job is unusually accurate about the verbs, because those are the verbs they use on a Tuesday.
  3. Next to each one, write honest yes or no. Not aspirational yes. If you have never sat in front of a board, the honest answer to present is no.
  4. For each no, find the closest verb that is true. You have not presented to a board but you have presented to a team. Say that, with that audience named.
  5. Now highlight the nouns. Tools, systems, sectors, customer types, regulations. This is the half most people skip, and it is the half a keyword comparison has any chance of matching, because verbs are not searchable terms and tools are.

Two things follow. The first is that a synonym is invisible to you and invisible to a filter. If the posting says stakeholder management and your resume says client relations, you have written the same experience twice and neither reader can see the connection. Fix that by using the posting's noun, but only for work you have actually done. The second is that matching the posting's verb matters far less than matching its noun. If the posting says spearhead and you have never used that word, nobody is searching for it.

This is also the natural moment to run the whole page against the description, because a lot of the words in your bullets are nouns rather than verbs. The quantifying impact guide covers the other half of the rewrite, because a strong verb with nothing measurable after it still leaves a reader guessing.

Words that look like content

Some of the worst text on any resume is not weak. It is impressive. Phrases like results-driven, dynamic professional, proven track record of success, team player and go-getter are written in the shape of a claim and carry none of one.

There is a quick test. Cover the sentence and ask what a stranger would see if you did that work for a day. Results-driven produces no picture. Reduced the time to close the month-end pack from six days to two produces a very clear one. The first is a claim about a personality; the second is a claim about the world, and only the second can be checked.

You will also see employer banlists quoted online, and they are a useful sanity check and a poor specification. The reason those words are weak is that they carry no information about what you did, and that stays true no matter whose list they appear on. Judge a word by whether a stranger could picture the resulting action, not by whether it has been declared out of bounds by a company you have never worked for.

The same five words, doing actual work
Adjectives

A highly motivated, results-driven team player with a proven track record of delivering dynamic solutions and building synergy across departments.

Actions
  • Delivered a shared intake process across four departments, replacing four separate request forms with one queue.
  • Trained eleven new starters on the system over two quarters.
  • Cut the time to answer a routine request from three days to same day.

The sentence on the left is 27 words and contains nothing a reader can use or ask about. The three lines on the right cover less ground and answer three separate questions: what, how much, and how. Neither version is more impressive to write. Only one of them is worth a line on a page you have one of.

Questions about wording

Should every bullet start with a different verb?

No, and forcing it is a mistake. Repeat a verb when the work genuinely was the same, which is common in operations, care, and support roles where the real achievement is doing the same hard thing many times. Vary the verb when the work varied. A section where every bullet opens with a different grand verb is usually a section where the writer was optimising the appearance of the page rather than describing the job.

Do action verbs actually matter to an applicant tracking system?

Not in any way anyone can demonstrate, and the honest answer is better than the usual one. No vendor publishes a keyword list, a verb weighting, or a ranking rule. Greenhouse documents that its Talent Matching feature does not extract fields beyond skills, job titles, years of experience, employment start and end dates, and company names. So the verbs are for the human who reads the page. The nouns, the tools, and the numbers are what any automated comparison has a chance of matching.

Is 'responsible for' ever acceptable on a resume?

Almost never. It describes who was given the work rather than what happened, and it occupies the same six characters as a verb that would have told the reader something. Responsible for managing a team and Managed a team of six report the same fact at different levels of usefulness, and the second one lets a reader compare you against someone who managed forty. If you genuinely owned a large budget or a whole function, say that instead, and use the space to say what changed.

Should the current job use present tense?

Yes. The current role is written in the present tense, and every role before it in the past tense. It is a small signal but it is a reliable one, because it tells the reader where your attention is. The exception is a capability that belongs to the current role and is written in a different section, such as a skills line, where a bare infinitive or the present tense is the normal form.

Do employers publish lists of words they do not want to see?

Some large employers have, and those lists get quoted and requoted online. They are a useful sanity check and a poor specification. The reason words like synergy, dynamic or results-driven are weak is that they carry no information about what you did, and that stays true no matter whose list they appear on. Judge a word by whether a stranger could picture the resulting action, not by whether it appears on a list.

How do I pick a verb for work I barely remember?

Choose from the artefact rather than from the memory. The report you wrote, the process you owned, the clients you looked after, the system you ran and the person you trained are all things you can name without remembering a single number. Pick the most accurate verb that describes the artefact, then add what the artefact was for. The result is specific, checkable, and true, which is more than most bullets on most resumes manage.
