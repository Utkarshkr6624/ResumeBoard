Guide 05

When to use a two-column resume layout

This is the only guide in the set that argues for breaking a rule, so it argues against it first. A sidebar looks considered, gives the skills list somewhere to live, and frees horizontal room for long date ranges. It also breaks a parsing step that two major vendors document by name. Here is the honest version of that trade, the three cases where the sidebar wins, and a rule you can apply in about half a minute.

A one-column resume is not the default because single columns look plainer. It is the default because it is the only layout a person and a piece of software agree to read in the same order. Everything below is either an argument about when that agreement is not needed, or a way of getting the look without the disagreement.

Why people want a sidebar

It is worth naming the real reasons, because each one has a single-column answer and the reasons are usually good. People reach for two columns because a narrow rail down the left looks considered, and on a page of plain text it genuinely is the difference between a document and a form. Because the skills list has nowhere else to go once the experience section has filled a page, and a paragraph of comma-separated tools looks like the thing it is. Because right-aligning dates against a wide single column leaves a lot of white space in the middle of the line, which some people read as unfinished.

None of those are silly reasons. Two of them are solved better in one column, and the third is a preference that you are entitled to hold. The problem is not the taste. It is that the layout decision is also a decision about how your file will be read by software, and that part is not a preference at all.

What the second column does to the text

A PDF is a set of instructions for drawing a page. To get anything out of it, something has to decide the order the lines were meant to be read in, and a two-column page removes the cue that decides it. A reader uses position: left before right, top before bottom. That cue still exists, but it now points in two directions at once, and it is the parser's problem rather than yours.

The two vendors that publish failure lists both name it. Greenhouse lists "resumes that have a columned layout" among the formatting that causes a partial parse. Workday's published guidelines say to "avoid tabular and column formats." Greenhouse is also specific that a parse "might be partially successful" — some fields filled, some skipped — and that this needs manual correction. That is the failure mode to plan for: not a rejection, a half-empty profile.

Now consider what a text extractor does with the first version. If it reads down the left rail before crossing to the right, the output opens with an email address, a phone number, a link, two languages and four tools, and your name does not appear at all, because in that version it is not on the page. If it reads across the page a line at a time, the output interleaves "English, Portuguese" with "Supply Chain Operations Manager", and the skills and the jobs become one paragraph.

Both outcomes are plausible, both are outside your control, and neither is visible to you in a PDF viewer. In the second version there is no decision to get wrong: the order on the screen is the order in the file. That is the entire argument for one column, and it is not a matter of taste.

What this does not mean

It does not mean a two-column file is certainly discarded, and you should be sceptical of anyone who says it is. No vendor publishes an accuracy rate, a ranking rule, or a rejection threshold. What is documented is a named failure mode with a named outcome: a partial parse. The honest position is that you would be taking a documented risk in exchange for an aesthetic gain, and that the gain is smaller than the risk.

The three cases where it holds up

There are three, and all three share one property: you know who is reading.

One: you know a named person is reading it

A referral, a recruiter you have spoken to, a hiring manager you met at something, a contact emailing you directly. In this case the file is not going into a database; it is going into an inbox and being read by somebody who already has a reason to read it. The documented parse risk does not apply, because there is no parse.

Two honest caveats. First, documents get forwarded. The person you send it to may paste the text into their own system, at which point a column that read well on screen becomes a column in a database. Second, the conversion of this situation is low, which is exactly why the design is safe: you are optimising for a conversation you are already having, not for volume.

Two: it is not a resume

Some documents that share a resume's vocabulary do not share its constraints. An academic or research CV with a rail of publications, teaching, and supervision is a genre with its own long-standing convention, and the sidebar is part of that convention rather than an innovation in it. The same is true of a creative one-pager sent alongside a portfolio, where the page exists to direct attention to the work rather than to be parsed into fields.

The test is whether the document's purpose is to be scanned into a form. If it is not, the usual reason for one column does not apply, and you can design for the reader in front of you.

Three: the second column holds no qualification

This is the narrowest version and the one most people could adopt safely. A true margin note carries contact details, a link, a language pair, and a one-line tools list. Nothing that qualifies you exists only there. The main column stands up as a complete resume on its own, and the rail is a convenience that can be ignored or lost without any consequence.

Notice what has to be true for this to work: the main column must be complete. If the only copy of a skill is in the rail, then the rail is load bearing, and a partial parse loses it. The rail is a margin note or it is not.

The decision rule

Three questions. All three have to be yes. The honest answer to question one is usually no, and that is the intended result.

Use two columns only if all three are yes
  • I know a person is reading this file, not a system. Not "I hope a person reads it." I know.
  • Nothing that qualifies me lives in the second column. I could delete it and the resume would lose nothing.
  • I could send the file tomorrow without changing it. No conversion, no re-layout, no second version I need to remember to make.

Two further habits make the rule sharper. Have a one-column version ready, because you will sometimes apply to a large organisation and sometimes to a person, and deciding at send time is much better than deciding at design time. And run the copy-and-paste test from the ATS formatting guide on any file you are genuinely unsure about, which takes two minutes and settles it.

Print, paper, and narrow measures

Most applications are now read on a screen, which is the argument people use for the sidebar. Paper is where the compromise becomes impossible to hide.

A rail spends ink on every page to repeat a name and an email address the reader already has from page one. The narrow measure it leaves for the bullets is worse than it sounds: a 34rem single column gives roughly 70 characters a line, which is comfortable, while a sidebar layout leaves a main column closer to 50, which is enough to force a second line in almost every bullet and turn a tidy list into a ragged one. And a resume is genuinely still printed in some sectors, and interviews are frequently conducted on a printed copy, where every colour separator between the columns has already vanished.

None of that is a disqualifier on its own. It is a list of costs that should be weighed against an aesthetic gain, rather than a reason the question cannot arise.

If you are going to do it anyway

Some people will read this and use two columns regardless, and that is a legitimate choice. Here is how to build one that degrades safely rather than one that fails silently.

  • Keep every qualification in the main column. Contact, links, a language pair, a short tools line. Nothing else. If a recruiter or parser loses the rail entirely, the resume must still be complete.
  • Keep all dates and employers in the main column. The rail is the worst possible place for employment history, because the two most fragile fields are the ones that need to stay attached to each other.
  • Make the rail narrow and the main column wide. If a bad extraction order happens, a wide main column survives as a coherent block. A design with two equal columns does not.
  • Put the name at the top of the main column, not in the rail. It is the first thing a person looks for and, per Workday's published guidance, the first thing a parser is looking for. Do not put it in the position that is hardest to reach.
  • No text boxes, no tables, no background images. All three are named independently as parse failures by the two vendors that publish failure lists, and a two-column layout is a good moment to introduce none of them.
  • Keep a one-column version. Different version for a large organisation, same file for a person you know. This is the single most useful thing on the list, because it removes the need to predict.
  • Test it before you send it. Open, select all, copy, paste into a plain text editor, read it in order. Two minutes, and it converts the whole question from opinion into evidence.

With those seven in place, a two-column file is not reckless. It is a considered trade: you are accepting a documented parse risk in exchange for a design you prefer, having made sure the consequences of losing the rail are nil. That is a defensible position. Sending a resume with the skills list in the rail, to an organisation you have never spoken to anyone at, is not.

One column by default

Structure decides whether content gets seen and parsing decides whether content survives. You can read resume basics for the structure, the ATS formatting guide for the parse, and the action verbs and quantifying impact guides for the content that ends up inside whatever layout you choose.

Questions about two columns

Is a two-column resume ever a good idea?

Yes, in three situations, and they are the only three. You know a named person is reading the file rather than a system. The document is not a resume in the normal sense, such as an academic or research CV with a publications list, or a creative one-pager sent alongside a portfolio. Or the second column is a true margin note that holds no qualification of its own, so the main column stands up on its own. In every other case the documented cost is a real parse risk and the benefit is an aesthetic one, which is not a trade most people should make.

Will a two-column resume get me filtered out automatically?

Nobody can honestly promise you either way, and you should distrust anyone who does. No vendor publishes an automatic rejection rule. What is published is narrower and still decisive: Greenhouse lists resumes that have a columned layout among the formatting that breaks a parse, and Workday's guidelines say to avoid tabular and column formats. So the documented risk is not rejection. It is a partial or empty set of profile fields, which is a worse outcome in some ways because nobody is told it happened.

What about a narrow skills column down the side?

That is the worst version of the idea, because skills are exactly what a parser is looking for and exactly what a sidebar is most likely to detach from your employment history. A skills list in the second column is the one block of the page that has no employer, no dates, and no verbs, so when the columns interleave it becomes a floating string of nouns in the middle of your career. Put it below the header as two or three grouped lines instead, where it is attached to you and where the reader sees it in the first second.

Does a two-column resume print properly?

It prints, and it is the medium where the compromise is clearest. A sidebar spends ink on every page to repeat a name and an email address that a reader already has. The narrow measure it leaves for your bullets is where the eye does the most work, and 40 to 60 words a line is harder going than the 70 a single column gives you. And if it is printed in monochrome, every colour-based separator between your columns disappears, so the two blocks sit side by side as an undifferentiated wall of text.

I work in design and every portfolio piece uses two columns. Should mine?

The consistency argument is real and it is the strongest one for the creative case, because the audience has a shared visual expectation and a two-column page reads as native to it. It is an argument about the audience, not about the format, so it only applies where the audience is the people you think it is. Test it: are these applications read by a designer or hiring manager who will look at the page, or are they going through a form? If the answer is that you do not know, use one column and spend the design effort somewhere it survives both routes, such as type, spacing and hierarchy.

Can I get the designed look without a second column?

Yes, and most of the time it looks better. What makes a resume look designed is hierarchy, alignment and restraint, none of which require a grid: a generous top margin, your name set larger than everything else, a single rule under the header, section headings in one consistent position, dates aligned to a consistent right-hand edge, and bullets in one style throughout. That language survives parsing, survives printing, survives a screen reader, and survives a recruiter reading it on a phone in a car park.
