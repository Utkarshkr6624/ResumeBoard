Guide 01

Resume basics: the structure recruiters expect

A resume is read in a fixed order, and almost nobody deviates from it. This guide covers that order, what belongs in each part, how long the document should be, and which sections you can delete without a second thought.

Most resume advice is about wording, and wording is the third-order problem. The first-order problem is whether a reader looking at your page for six seconds finds what they came for. That is a question of structure, and structure is easier to get right than prose.

The order readers use

When someone opens a resume they do not start at the top and read down. They look at the name, they look for the most recent role, they look for a match to the job they are hiring for, and they look for the current title. Everything else gets read only if those four checks pass. This is not a stylistic habit. It is a triage behaviour, and it happens whether the reader is a person or a piece of software.

That behaviour produces a default reading path, and the strongest resumes are built to serve it:

  1. Your name, so the reader can file the document.
  2. Your current title, because that is the single fastest signal of fit.
  3. Your most recent entry, which is where the evidence for that title lives.
  4. Your contact details, which they look for only once they are interested.

Contact details last feels wrong, and for years it was standard to put them at the very top in a tight block. It has stopped mattering. Nobody calls you because of your email address, and a header that is mostly punctuation pushes the parts that matter further down. The exception is a phone number for a role worked over the phone, or a country where a specific contact format is conventional.

The short version
  • Name, current title, most recent role, contact details. That is the reading path.
  • Everything else is supporting evidence, ordered by how much the reader needs it.
  • Structure is a filter. A weak structure hides good content from a reader who would have valued it.

The header block

The header is four pieces of information, and the commonest mistake is padding it into eight. A name, a current title, a location, a contact line, and a link if you have something worth linking to. That is the whole thing.

A header that spends five lines on one fact
Padded

Jordan A. Reyes-Mbeki

Senior Manager, Supply Chain Operations & Continuous Improvement
Full-Time Professional / Open to Relocation
Email: jordan.reyes.mbeki@fastmail.example
Mobile: +44 7700 900412
LinkedIn: linkedin.com/in/jordanreyesmbeki
Portfolio: jreyes.example
Reference available on request

Functional

Jordan Reyes-Mbeki

Supply Chain Operations Manager
Manchester, United Kingdom
jordan.reyes.mbeki@fastmail.example · +44 7700 900412
jreyes.example

Both versions carry the same facts. The second one carries them in four lines instead of seven, and it does not tell a stranger that the candidate is open to relocation, which is a conversation, not a qualification. Notice also that the second version puts the title directly under the name. That single line is doing more work than everything else in the block combined.

One more decision belongs here: whether the title should be your formal job title or a description of the role you want. Use the formal title when it is recognisable and relevant. Use a descriptive title when your actual title is internal, obscure, or does not exist in the market, as it often does in small companies. Logistics Lead communicates more than Level 2 Operations Specialist, and it does not misdescribe what you do.

Which sections, in which order

There is a conventional order, and it exists because it is the order that resolves the most questions fastest. If you are not sure, use it:

The conventional order of resume sections
OrderSectionWhat belongs in it
1Contact headerName, current title, location, contact line, one link
2Summary or headlineOne line at most, only if your title alone is not enough
3ExperienceRoles, most recent first, with the evidence for each
4Projects or selected workFor portfolios, side projects, or contract work
5EducationDegree, institution, year. Detail matters for research and teaching roles
6SkillsGrouped by kind, not a single undifferentiated list of forty items
7Certifications and trainingOnly where the credential is recognised or required

Education sits below experience by default, and that ordering is not a statement about importance. It reflects the fact that a reader with ten years of evidence does not need your degree to make a decision, while a recent graduate does. If you have less than two years of relevant experience, move education above experience. If you are applying to a research, medical, or academic post, put education and publications near the top regardless of experience, because in those fields the degree is the qualification.

Drop any section you have nothing to put in. An empty skills section, a hobbies section with two lines in it, and a references section all make the document look thinner than it is. A section exists to answer a question; if it answers nothing, it is just taking up the room where an entry could be.

Renaming a section

Standard headings are standard because they are legible at a glance and because parsers recognise them. You can still rename them when the standard name is vague or wrong for your field, as long as the new name is honest about what is inside.

A heading that describes the container
Before

Work Experience

Distribution Centre Supervisor Mar 2021 – Present

Northgate Logistics, Leeds

After

Operations and Delivery

Distribution Centre Supervisor Mar 2021 – Present

Northgate Logistics, Leeds

Operations and Delivery tells a logistics reader more in two words than Work Experience tells them in two, and it still contains jobs. What you should not do is rename Work Experience to a phrase containing a keyword from the posting when your entries are something else. That is not editing, and it is the fastest way to lose an interview in a screening call.

Reverse chronology, with two rules

Everything inside a section runs newest to oldest, because the reader starts at the top looking for the most recent evidence. Two rules make that work, and people break both of them constantly.

Rule one: no unexplained gaps. If your dates read 2019 to 2022 and then 2016 to 2019 with nothing between, a reader will assume you are hiding something rather than that you were caring for a parent or doing a degree. The fix is not a lie. A line reading 2019–2022 · Career break or Masters, University of Leeds costs you nothing and removes the question from the reader's head. A gap is only a problem while it is unexplained.

Rule two: overlapping dates should mean something. Contract work alongside a permanent role is common and legitimate. Two full-time roles at once is a different story, and if it happened, say so: Contract, four days a week is honest and it is also a useful fact about how you work. Dates that overlap without explanation read as an error, and readers correct errors by discarding.

Keep the format identical everywhere

Pick one date style and never deviate: Mar 2021 – Present throughout, or 03/2021 – current throughout. Mixed styles are the single most common cause of a parser misreading a timeline, and they are covered in detail in the ATS formatting guide.

The anatomy of an entry

A job entry has four parts: the role, the employer, the location, the dates, and then the bullets. The first four should occupy one or two lines and be scannable without reading a single word of prose. The bullets carry the evidence.

The most useful constraint here is the three-line test. Read only the first three lines of any entry on your resume, as a skimming reader would. If those lines do not tell a stranger roughly what you are good for, the entry is not doing its job, no matter how good the fifth bullet is.

An entry that needs a full read to understand
Duties
Various roles, increasing responsibility 2016 – now

A retail company, then a charity, then a university

  • Worked on the shop floor and then in the back office.
  • Handled customer queries and complaints.
  • Was asked to look after the volunteers, which I did for a while.
Evidence
Volunteer Coordinator Aug 2019 – Jun 2021

City Mission, Bristol

  • Coordinated 60 volunteers across six sites, replacing a rota that had been maintained by hand.
  • Cut no-shows by a third by moving confirmation to a two-day-ahead text message.
  • Rebuilt the emergency contact list, which had not been verified in four years.

The rewrite here is mostly a decision about which job to feature. Three jobs collapsed into one line reads as three jobs in a hat, and the reader concludes you have not held a role long enough to be trusted with one. Naming the one that was genuinely yours, at its actual title, with actual dates, makes the rest of the document believable.

Bullet count is the other common argument. Four to six bullets per role is the useful range for a current or recent job. Older roles, and roles where you did less, should be shorter: one or two lines. A single bullet for a role you held for eighteen months is more credible than four, and it leaves room on the page for something you did more of.

How long it should be

Length is a consequence of scope, and it is worth resisting the urge to optimise for a page count. A page of dense, relevant evidence beats two pages of everything you have ever done, and so does the opposite: a two-page resume that is all relevant beats a one-page resume with a page of padding in the middle.

Recommended resume length by career stage
StageTargetReason
Student or career changerOne pageThere is little to show. Space makes thin content look thinner
Under five yearsOne pageEnough room for four or five entries with real evidence
Five to fifteen yearsOne or two pagesRange and variety need somewhere to go; two is acceptable
Fifteen years or moreTwo pages, focusedCompress early career, expand the last ten years
Research, medical, academicWhatever the discipline usesCV conventions override general advice, including length

Past roughly twenty years, stop trying to fit everything and start choosing. A focused two-page document covering the last twelve years plus a selective earlier role beats a five-page chronology. A screener needs enough to judge your current level and enough breadth to see range. Beyond that, older entries are read only when something on the first page specifically triggers a question about them.

What to cut

Most resumes are too long because nothing was ever removed. The following can almost always go, and removing them is faster than rewriting anything.

  • The objective statement. It is a paragraph about the job you want, which the reader already knows, and it sits where your most recent evidence should be.
  • A skills list longer than about twelve items. Beyond that it stops being a signal. Everything you list as a skill should also appear in an entry, where it is attached to something you did.
  • Duties that any person in the role would have done. If the job title already implies it, the bullet adds nothing. Attended team meetings is the classic example.
  • Full street addresses and postcodes. City and country is enough, and a full home address is an unnecessary disclosure.
  • Marital status, date of birth, nationality, religion, and a photo, unless the local convention expects them.
  • References, or a line saying they are available. Nobody has ever asked for this on the basis of seeing it written down.
  • Anything from before your target role began that is not relevant to it. A decade-old fine art degree is not evidence for a data role, and a relevant decade-old role is.

Five structural mistakes

  1. A summary that summarises the page. It repeats the title, the experience, and the skills, and it pushes all three down a screen.
  2. Chronological order. It is a legitimate format for a career with no gaps and an unusual story, and it is the wrong default everywhere else, because it makes the reader hunt for your current level.
  3. Bullets with no bullet in them. Entries that are one long sentence, or a sentence per line, break the visual rhythm the skimming reader depends on. If it is not a list, make it a list.
  4. Mixed date formats. 2021 in one place and Jan 2021 in another, or 03/21 in a country that reads it as March, causes real misreadings. Pick one.
  5. Empty sections kept for symmetry. A heading with nothing under it is a signal that you did not edit. Delete the heading.

Questions about structure

How long should a resume be?

One page up to roughly ten years of relevant experience, two pages after that, and a curated document rather than a full history once you are past twenty years. Length is a symptom, not a rule: if a page contains a line that helps nobody decide anything, cut the line, not the rest of the page.

Should I use reverse chronological order?

Almost always, for both the entries inside a section and the sections themselves. Recruiters scan the most recent thing first, so the most recent thing goes on top. The exception is a functional resume built around a specific target role, and even that has stopped being worth the parsing risk.

Do I need a summary or objective at the top?

No. A summary restates what the rest of the page already proves, and it occupies the most valuable real estate on the document. Use those four lines for your name, a current title, contact details and a link to your work instead. A cover letter is the right place for a statement of intent.

Should I include every job I have ever had?

Include the last ten to fifteen years, and include everything inside your target role rather than stopping at an arbitrary date. If an early job is genuinely relevant to the role you want, it can stay even if it is old. Roles that are neither recent nor relevant are the only ones that reliably belong in a separate, shorter document.

Can I rename sections to match the job posting?

Yes, as long as the title still describes what is inside. Operations and Delivery for a logistics role reads better than Work Experience, and parsers handle it. But Creative Synergies for a list of job titles does not work. Rename for accuracy and specificity, never to insert a keyword that is not true.

Do I need a photo, my date of birth, or my marital status?

Not in most markets, and you should not volunteer them. A photo introduces bias risk and can slow down parsing, and age, marital status and nationality are not relevant to whether you can do the job. In the countries where a photo is still conventional, follow the local expectation, but never let it push a real qualification off the page.
